Why This Map Exists

×

This data belongs to the American people, who need to know what is being done to our National Parks. This administration is trying to use our public lands to erase history and undermine science.

Executive Order 14253 “Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History” and Secretary Order 3431 directed parks to censor any content “that inappropriately disparages Americans…or that emphasizes matters unrelated to the beauty, abundance, or grandeur” of natural features.
Why Are They Censoring Science and History?

Dismantling trusted sources of science and history makes their agenda of lies easier. Profiting from coal and oil is a lot easier if the impacts of fossil fuels are censored at sites like Muir Woods, Glacier, Acadia, and Everglades.

It’s easier to illegally detain people if we forget the true stories of Japanese-American incarceration in World War II, told at national park sites like Manzanar and Minidoka and Amache and Tule Lake and Honouliuli.

Propping up systemic racism is easier if you hide the evidence of the atrocities of chattel slavery, Jim Crow, and white nationalism at park sites like Medgar and Myrlie Evers Home, Cane River Creole, Rock Creek, and Independence Hall.

Most of all, they want to turn the American people against their national parks. They want to discredit the national parks and set the stage to privatize them.

⚖️ Court Orders Park History Restored

Jun 23 – Jun 28, 2026 · 5 new developments
✅ Removal Ban Still in Force ⏸️ 1st Cir. Lifts July 4 Deadline ⚠️ President’s House: Appeals Setback

Where the Fight Stands

The legal fight over history in America’s national parks has split in two directions. In the nationwide case, U.S. District Judge Angel Kelley in Boston granted a preliminary injunction on June 12 (National Parks Conservation Association v. Department of the Interior, No. 1:26-cv-10877), ordering the Interior Department to restore the signs and exhibits it stripped from parks—on slavery, climate change, Indigenous and civil-rights history—by July 3, just ahead of the 250th anniversary. Judge Kelley wrote that the administration had tried to “rewrite the Nation’s history with a white-out pen.” On June 16 the government appealed to the First Circuit and asked her to pause the order; she refused—but on June 23 the First Circuit Court of Appeals stepped in and temporarily lifted the July 3 deadline, so the administration no longer has to reinstall everything before the 250th anniversary. The panel left the rest of Kelley’s injunction in place—the administration still may not remove more—and said it is weighing whether to pause her entire ruling during the appeal.

But on June 18, a separate case delivered a major setback. A unanimous panel of the Third Circuit Court of Appeals ruled the administration may remove and replace the “From Enslavement to Emancipation” exhibit at the President’s House in Philadelphia—the memorial to the nine people George Washington enslaved—tossing out the February district-court order that had required its restoration. Federal attorneys said replacement panels were already manufactured and ready to install. Mayor Cherelle Parker vowed to “pursue every legal action possible,” and Rep. Brendan Boyle urged Congress to pass his Protecting American History Act. The two rulings now point opposite ways: the park system at large must restore its displays, while the President’s House exhibit can be replaced.

The clash unfolds against mounting evidence that the administration’s own crowdsourced review backfired. An Associated Press analysis of roughly 35,000 public comments submitted through Interior’s QR-code feedback program found only 14—0.04 percent—called for removals, while more than half showed coordinated opposition to the effort, per the Center for Western Priorities. A court filing has already forced the administration to disclose its list of removed materials—from climate signs at Acadia to civil-rights content nationwide. With the July 4 deadline now lifted, the next test is whether the First Circuit pauses Kelley’s ruling entirely while the appeal plays out—and whether the removed exhibits go back up at all.
