NPS History

NPSHistory.com

Arlington House was built between 1803 and 1818 by George Washington Parke Custis, the grandson of Martha Washington, as a memorial to George Washington. His daughter, Mary Anna Custis, married Robert E. Lee there in 1831, and the couple lived at the estate for 30 years before Lee resigned his U.S. Army commission to join the Confederacy in 1861. Union forces occupied the property early in the Civil War, and in 1864 the grounds were designated as a military cemetery — the origin of Arlington National Cemetery. The mansion was designated a national memorial in 1955 and is managed by the National Park Service in partnership with Arlington National Cemetery.

Entry #775 REVISE

REVISE: Publications (1 items)

Publications (1 items) 0 photos

At Arlington House, the National Park Service has worked for years to document and interpret the history of slavery at this site — a history that was ignored for much of the park's existence. The exhibits, waysides, and publications now under review represent a hard-won commitment to telling the complete American story.

Among the content targeted: “In 1829, Robert E. Lee promised to serve in the Army and protect the United States. In 1861, he broke his promise and fought for slavery.” — language that the administration has ordered revised under its directive to review historically accurate interpretive materials.

Why this matters: The history of slavery at Arlington House was suppressed for generations. The materials now being targeted represent decades of work by historians, descendants of enslaved people, and community advocates to ensure these stories are finally told. Removing them would re-erase history that has only recently been acknowledged.
